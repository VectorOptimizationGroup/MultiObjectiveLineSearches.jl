BackTracking

BackTracking searches along a given direction for a step size that satisfies an Armijo-type sufficient decrease condition in every objective. Rejected steps are reduced by safeguarded quadratic interpolation.

Overview

Let $F=(F_1,\ldots,F_m):\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ be the objective map, let $x$ be the current point and let $d(x)$ be a search direction at $x$. Along $d(x)$, write

\[\phi_i(\alpha)=F_i(x+\alpha d(x)),\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.\]

BackTracking uses only the values $\phi_i(\alpha)$ returned by the caller's evaluator at trial steps, together with two inputs from the caller: a scalar decrease factor $\eta<0$ (factor) and one slope $s_i$ per objective (slopes). It computes no derivatives and makes no assumption about $F$, such as differentiability or convexity. The meaning of $\eta$ and $s_i$ is set by the method that calls the search; Armijo-type conditions in the literature shows the choices made in the cited works.

configure_linesearch converts the options to the element type T and checks that $0<c_1<1$, that $0<\tau_1\leq1/2\leq\tau_2<1$, that minstep, maxstep and the initial step are finite, that 0 < minstep < maxstep, and that minstep <= initial_step.alpha <= maxstep. Steps only decrease during a search, so no trial step exceeds maxstep.

Armijo-type condition

BackTracking accepts $\alpha>0$ when every $F_i(x+\alpha d(x))$ is finite and

\[F_i(x+\alpha d(x))\leq F_i(x)+c_1\alpha\eta,\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.\]

The same term $c_1\alpha\eta$ is used for all objectives. For $m=1$ and $\eta=\nabla F_1(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x)$, this is the Armijo condition of scalar optimization; see Nocedal and Wright [1], equation (3.4).

The multiobjective works cited here name their extensions of this condition differently: Fliege and Svaiter [2] call it an Armijo-like rule, Lucambio Pérez and Prudente [3] the Armijo condition for vector optimization, and Assunção, Ferreira and Prudente [4] an Armijo-type step size. This documentation uses Armijo-type condition.

When $F$ is differentiable, the generalized directional derivative of $F$ at $x$ along $d(x)$ is

\[\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\nabla F_j(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x),\]

see [3]. If $\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))<0$, then $d(x)$ is a descent direction for every objective.

BackTracking does not verify that the condition can be satisfied: with an arbitrary negative factor, the search may end with :minimum_step or :max_iterations. For each condition in Armijo-type conditions in the literature, the cited work shows that, under its assumptions, all sufficiently small steps are accepted.

Armijo-type conditions in the literature

The works below differ in the decrease term on the right-hand side of the condition. In all of them, $c_1\in(0,1)$.

One slope per objective

Fliege and Svaiter [2], Section 4, assume that $F$ is differentiable and that $\nabla F_i(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x)<0$ for all $i$, and accept $\alpha$ when

\[F_i(x+\alpha d(x))\leq F_i(x)+c_1\alpha\nabla F_i(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x), \qquad i=1,\ldots,m,\]

their equation (7). The search starts at $\alpha=1$ and halves $\alpha$ until (7) holds. Their Lemma 4 shows that (7) holds strictly for all sufficiently small $\alpha>0$.

Each objective has its own slope $s_i=\nabla F_i(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x)$ on the right-hand side. BackTracking does not implement this rule, since it uses one scalar $\eta$ for all objectives. With $\eta=\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))$ we have $s_i\leq\eta$, so (7) implies the BackTracking condition; the converse does not hold in general.

The generalized directional derivative

Lucambio Pérez and Prudente [3] work in vector optimization, where vectors are compared through the partial order induced by a cone. In the multiobjective case this order is componentwise, and their Armijo condition, equation (6), reduces to equation (7) of Fliege and Svaiter [2]. Their Definition 3.1 introduces Wolfe conditions for vector optimization. With the choices the authors indicate for multiobjective problems, the sufficient decrease part of these conditions, (7a) and (8a), uses the generalized directional derivative as the common term:

\[F_i(x+\alpha d(x))\leq F_i(x)+c_1\alpha\mathcal{D}(x,d(x)), \qquad i=1,\ldots,m.\]

This is the BackTracking condition with $\eta=\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))$. The proof of their Proposition 3.2 shows that it holds for all sufficiently small $\alpha>0$ when $F$ is continuously differentiable, $\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))<0$ and $F$ is bounded below along $d(x)$. BackTracking checks only this decrease condition, not the curvature conditions (7b) or (8b).

Composite objectives

Assunção, Ferreira and Prudente [4] consider objectives $F_i=f_i+h_i$, where $f_i$ is continuously differentiable and $h_i$ is proper, convex and lower semicontinuous. At an iterate $x$, their generalized conditional gradient method computes, in equations (14) and (15),

\[\begin{aligned} p(x)&\in\operatorname*{argmin}_{u}\max_{1\leq j\leq m} \{h_j(u)-h_j(x)+\nabla f_j(x)^\mathsf{T}(u-x)\},\\ \theta(x)&=\max_{1\leq j\leq m} \{h_j(p(x))-h_j(x)+\nabla f_j(x)^\mathsf{T}(p(x)-x)\}, \end{aligned}\]

and uses the direction $d(x)=p(x)-x$. By their Lemma 3.1, $\theta(x)\leq0$, with equality if and only if $x$ is Pareto critical. Their Armijo-type step size (Section 4, steps LS0–LS2) starts at $\alpha=1$, accepts $\alpha$ when

\[F_i(x+\alpha d(x))\leq F_i(x)-c_1\alpha|\theta(x)|, \qquad i=1,\ldots,m,\]

and otherwise chooses the next step in $[\tau_1\alpha,\tau_2\alpha]$, with $0<\tau_1<\tau_2<1$. This is the BackTracking condition with $\eta=-|\theta(x)|$. Their Proposition 4.1 shows that, when $\theta(x)<0$, the condition holds for all $\alpha$ in an interval $(0,\bar\alpha]$ with $\bar\alpha\leq1$. With the default options, every trial step lies in $(0,1]$, the range of step sizes in their Algorithm 1. Every reduction made by BackTracking lies in $[\tau_1\alpha,\tau_2\alpha]$; see Quadratic interpolation and safeguards. The defaults $c_1=10^{-4}$, $\tau_1=0.05$ and $\tau_2=0.95$ are the values reported in their Section 5, where the line search is coded by quadratic interpolation of the coordinate functions.

The objectives $F_i$ need not be differentiable here, so the slopes used for interpolation are chosen by the caller, for example the directional derivatives $\nabla f_i(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x)$ of the differentiable parts.

Quadratic interpolation and safeguards

Suppose a trial with finite values is rejected, and let $j$ be the first index, in increasing order, whose condition fails. With the current step $\alpha$ and the caller's slope $s_j$, BackTracking forms

\[q_j(t)=\phi_j(0)+s_jt+b_jt^2, \qquad b_j=\frac{\phi_j(\alpha)-\phi_j(0)-\alpha s_j}{\alpha^2},\]

so that $q_j(0)=\phi_j(0)$, $q_j'(0)=s_j$ and $q_j(\alpha)=\phi_j(\alpha)$. The stationary point of $q_j$ is

\[\widehat\alpha =-\frac{s_j\alpha^2} {2\,[\phi_j(\alpha)-\phi_j(0)-\alpha s_j]}.\]

The new step is $\widehat\alpha$ if $s_j<0$, the denominator is finite and nonzero, $\widehat\alpha$ is finite, and

\[\tau_1\alpha\leq\widehat\alpha\leq\tau_2\alpha,\]

where $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ are the options tau_1 and tau_2.

In that case $\widehat\alpha>0$ and $s_j<0$ imply $b_j>0$, so $\widehat\alpha$ is the minimizer of $q_j$. Otherwise the new step is $\alpha/2$: a proposal outside the interval is replaced, not clamped to it. If some trial value is NaN or infinite, the step is halved without interpolation. Since $\tau_1\leq1/2\leq\tau_2$, halving also lies in the interval, so every reduction gives a new step in $[\tau_1\alpha,\tau_2\alpha]$; with the defaults, between 5% and 95% of the previous one. With tau_1 = tau_2 = 0.5, every reduction halves the step, as in the search of Fliege and Svaiter [2].

When $s_j=\phi_j'(0)$, $q_j$ and $\widehat\alpha$ are the quadratic interpolant and its minimizer in Nocedal and Wright [1], Section 3.5, equations (3.57) and (3.58). Otherwise $q_j$ still matches the two values and the slope $s_j$. The slopes only affect the proposed steps; the acceptance test uses the objective values and $\eta$.

Nocedal and Wright continue with cubic interpolation after the first quadratic trial, and reset a trial to half its predecessor when it is too close to or too much smaller than that predecessor. BackTracking uses the quadratic model at every reduction of a trial with finite values, with the interval above. Choosing the first violating objective and the fallback rules above are choices of this implementation; the cited works do not prescribe them.

Trial sequence and stopping

Starting from alpha = initial_step.alpha:

  1. Form $x+\alpha d(x)$. If it equals $x$ in floating-point arithmetic, return :indistinguishable_point without evaluating it.
  2. Evaluate the objectives. If the evaluator reports an error, return :evaluation_failure.
  3. If all values are finite and satisfy the Armijo-type condition, return :success.
  4. If max_iters reductions have been made, return :max_iterations.
  5. Reduce the step: halve it if some value is not finite, otherwise apply the safeguarded quadratic proposal.
  6. If the new step is at or below minstep, return :minimum_step without evaluating it. Otherwise go to step 1.

With max_iters=0, only the initial step is evaluated. An initial step equal to minstep is evaluated, since the threshold applies to reduced steps. The limits max_iters and minstep, the rounding check and the handling of nonfinite values belong to this implementation; they are not part of the line searches of Fliege and Svaiter [2] or of Assunção, Ferreira and Prudente [4].

Usage

Consider $F=(F_1,F_2):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2$ with

\[F_1(x)=(x-1)^2,\qquad F_2(x)=(x-1)^2+1,\]

the current point $x=0$ and the direction $d(x)=3$. Then $F(0)=(1,2)$ and $\nabla F_1(0)=\nabla F_2(0)=-2$, so the slopes are $s_i=\nabla F_i(x)^\mathsf{T}d(x)=-6$. The caller passes $\eta=\mathcal{D}(x,d(x))=-6$, the choice of Lucambio Pérez and Prudente [3].

The first trial, $\alpha=1$, gives $F(3)=(4,5)$ and is rejected by $F_1$. Since $F_1$ is quadratic and $s_1=\phi_1'(0)$, the model $q_1$ equals $\phi_1$, and its minimizer $\widehat\alpha=1/3$ is accepted with $F(1)=(0,1)$. In the code, objectives! fills values with $F(x)$.

julia> using MultiObjectiveLineSearches

julia> function objectives!(values, x)
           values[1] = (x[1] - 1)^2
           values[2] = (x[1] - 1)^2 + 1
           return nothing
       end;

julia> x = [0.0]; d = [3.0]; Fx = [1.0, 2.0]; slopes = [-6.0, -6.0];

julia> search = configure_linesearch(BackTracking(), Float64);

julia> x_trial = similar(x); F_trial = similar(Fx);

julia> result = perform_linesearch!(search, objectives!, x, d, Fx, slopes,
                                  x_trial, F_trial; factor=maximum(slopes));

julia> (result.success, result.nfev, result.iterations, result.alpha ≈ 1/3)
(true, 2, 1, true)

julia> (x_trial ≈ [1.0], F_trial ≈ [0.0, 1.0], x == [0.0])
(true, true, true)

For the evaluator contract and buffer ownership, see Using a line search.

Method reference

MultiObjectiveLineSearches.BackTracking — Type
BackTracking(; initial_step=InitialStatic(), c_1=1e-4, tau_1=0.05, tau_2=0.95,
             max_iters=1000, minstep=1e-15, maxstep=1.0, verbose=0)

Backtracking line search for an Armijo-type sufficient decrease condition, with safeguarded quadratic interpolation.

An interpolated step is used only if it lies in [tau_1*alpha, tau_2*alpha], where alpha is the rejected step; otherwise the step is halved. max_iters limits the number of step reductions; zero allows only the initial trial. Set verbose=1 to log the final diagnostic with @info.

configure_linesearch checks the options after conversion: 0 < c_1 < 1, 0 < tau_1 <= 1/2 <= tau_2 < 1, finite 0 < minstep < maxstep, an initial step in [minstep, maxstep], max_iters >= 0 and verbose equal to 0 or 1.

See BackTracking for the condition, its sources and the reduction rules.

perform_linesearch!(search, evaluate!, x, d, Fx, slopes,

Run a configured line search and return a LineSearchResult. On success, the trial buffers hold the accepted point and objective values; on failure, their contents are not an accepted point.
